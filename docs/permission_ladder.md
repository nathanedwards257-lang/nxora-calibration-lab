# Permission Ladder

## Introduction

Most assessment systems focus on detection.

They attempt to identify signals that may indicate quality, understanding, reasoning, judgement, or evaluation.

Detection is important.

However, detection alone does not solve the harder problem:

**How much authority should detected evidence possess?**

The Permission Ladder is a public governance framework developed to explore that question.

Rather than treating all detected evidence as immediately influential, the framework proposes that evidence should earn progressively higher levels of trust before it gains decision-making authority.

The Permission Ladder is not a scoring system.

It is an authority-allocation framework.

Its purpose is to govern how evidence acquires influence within a decision environment.

This document presents the concept at a philosophical level only.

It does not disclose private calibration systems, scoring thresholds, examiner materials, protected datasets, or internal engine logic.

---

# The Authority Problem

Many systems successfully identify signals.

The more difficult challenge is determining whether those signals deserve trust.

A detected signal may appear persuasive while providing little genuine evidence.

A phrase may look analytical.

A statement may sound evaluative.

A response may contain technically correct terminology.

None of these observations automatically establish reliability.

When systems grant authority too early, several problems can emerge:

* superficial pattern reward
* false confidence
* unstable decision making
* overreaction to isolated signals
* inconsistent outcomes
* reduced transparency

The Authority Problem asks a simple question:

**When should evidence be permitted to influence important decisions?**

The Permission Ladder was developed as one possible answer.

---

# Why Detection Is Not Enough

Observation and authority are not the same thing.

A signal can exist without being trustworthy.

A signal can be trustworthy without being decisive.

A signal can be decisive only after sufficient confidence has been established.

The framework therefore separates three distinct ideas:

* detection
* trust
* authority

Many systems focus heavily on the first.

The Permission Ladder focuses on the relationship between all three.

---

# The Four Permission Levels

## P0 — Observed

A signal has been detected.

The system recognises that something may be present.

No judgement has yet been made regarding quality, reliability, significance, or authority.

Observation represents awareness only.

---

## P1 — Validated

The signal has survived basic verification.

The evidence appears genuine rather than accidental, superficial, or misleading.

Validation establishes that a signal deserves consideration.

It does not yet grant meaningful influence.

---

## P2 — Trusted

The evidence demonstrates sufficient consistency and credibility to be regarded as reliable.

The signal is no longer treated as an isolated observation.

Instead, it becomes part of a broader body of trustworthy evidence.

Trust is earned through quality rather than repetition.

---

## P3 — Authorised

The evidence has acquired sufficient confidence to influence higher-level decisions.

Authority is granted only after earlier stages have been satisfied.

The evidence is no longer merely observed or trusted.

It is authorised to participate in decision-making processes.

---

# Core Governance Principles

## Principle 1: Observation Is Not Authority

Detection alone does not justify influence.

The existence of evidence is distinct from the authority of evidence.

---

## Principle 2: Trust Must Be Earned

Authority should increase only as confidence increases.

Evidence should not receive influence by default.

---

## Principle 3: Stronger Decisions Require Stronger Permission

The greater the consequence of a decision, the greater the burden placed on supporting evidence.

Important decisions require stronger justification than minor ones.

---

## Principle 4: Evidence Should Remain Auditable

The progression from observation to authority should remain visible and reviewable.

Transparency improves reliability and accountability.

---

## Principle 5: Authority Should Be Proportional

Evidence should receive influence appropriate to its demonstrated reliability.

Neither under-weighting nor over-weighting evidence supports robust decision making.

---

# Example: Evaluation Language

Consider a system that detects evaluative language.

Detection alone confirms only that evaluation-like wording exists.

It does not automatically establish that meaningful evaluation has occurred.

Under a permission-based approach:

* detection creates observation
* verification creates validation
* demonstrated reliability creates trust
* sufficient confidence creates authority

The key idea is that influence is earned rather than assumed.

---

# Relationship to Evidence Governance

The Permission Ladder should not be viewed as an evaluation framework.

It is an evidence-governance framework.

Its role is not to determine whether something is good or bad.

Its role is to regulate how evidence acquires authority.

In this sense, the Permission Ladder operates as a governance layer positioned between observation and decision making.

The framework helps ensure that evidence receives influence only after appropriate levels of confidence have been established.

---

# Broader Applications

Although developed within an assessment context, the underlying concept is broadly applicable.

Potential applications include:

* educational assessment
* quality assurance systems
* auditing frameworks
* compliance review processes
* decision-support systems
* risk assessment environments
* governance structures
* artificial intelligence oversight

In each case, the same principle applies:

**Detection should not automatically become authority.**

Authority should be earned through demonstrated trustworthiness.

---

# Conclusion

The Permission Ladder represents a structured approach to evidence authority.

Rather than allowing detected signals to immediately influence decisions, the framework proposes a progression from observation to validation, trust, and eventual authorisation.

By separating evidence detection from evidence authority, systems may become more transparent, more auditable, and more resistant to error.

The framework does not attempt to determine what should be trusted.

Instead, it establishes a structure through which trust may be earned before meaningful influence is granted.
